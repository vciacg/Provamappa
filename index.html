This number isn't a probability: it's a composite index that summarizes upper-level wind, air instability and gusts along the route on a 0–100 scale. This is the weather at ground level (the lowest ~2 meters): it can be calm and clear even when, at cruising altitude (9–10 km above the same spot), there's strong wind or instability — they're two different layers of air. That's exactly why "clear-air" turbulence can catch you by surprise even under a blue sky.

How strong the wind is, and how much it changes with altitude, around 9–10 km, where airliners fly. It's the average across 5 points sampled along the whole route, not just at the airports: a single rough stretch can raise it even if the rest is calm.
Air instability — CAPE (route average)

Energy available for updrafts and storms. It's the average across 5 points sampled along the whole route (not the weather at departure or arrival): one unstable stretch mid-flight can raise it even with fine weather at both airports.

Time Level Index
Read this before flying calmly: even a "high" score on this scale is almost always just uncomfortable, not dangerous: aircraft are built to withstand far stronger stresses than the turbulence normally encountered, and pilots and controllers monitor and avoid it when they can. This is an indicative estimate from public weather models, not an official aviation forecast.
The "vs. seasonal average" comparisons are based on the same days of the year in past years (from 2022 onward, when available) at the route's midpoint: they give a sense of how much the forecast situation differs from what's typical for that area, not an official airline figure. Detailed weather forecasts only cover roughly the next 14 days, though: beyond that window the score and comparisons won't be available.
